\begin{abstract}
Camouflaged object detection needs pixel-accurate masks that are slow and costly to draw, so
synthetic training images are a standard remedy. That raises a question nobody has answered: under a
fixed budget, which images should you generate? The obvious answer is to let the model choose, and
we built that loop --- cluster the unlabelled real images the model will face, find the clusters it
is least sure about, aim the generation there, retrain, repeat.

No advantage survives measurement. Across $103$ trained runs, targeting does not beat random
allocation, and five independent controls each close a different reason that null might be an
artifact. The reason is itself a positive finding: a targeted training set
\emph{is} measurably different from a random one, but the difference comes from concentrating the
budget rather than aiming it --- every rule that preserves the allocation's shape reproduces the
effect, including one that reverses the uncertainty signal outright. Separately, $50$ of
CHAMELEON's $76$ images are re-encoded copies of training data, while the standard check
reports $0$ overlap.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Camouflaged object detection asks for a pixel-accurate outline of an animal that has evolved not to
have one. Its training masks are unusually expensive for exactly that reason: an annotator must
decide where a boundary lies when the point of the object is that its boundary is invisible. One
standard response to the resulting scarcity is to manufacture more data --- a generator takes a real
cut-out object and paints a fresh background behind it, producing a new labelled pair at no
annotation cost.

Generation is not free either, though, and every generated image has to be trained on. So if you can
afford a thousand of them, which thousand? The obvious answer is to let the model choose: train it
on what you have, look at the unlabelled real images it will actually be judged on, find the ones it
handles least confidently, and aim the generation there. Every ingredient of that loop is
standard --- uncertainty sampling from active learning~\citep{settles2009active}, a student--teacher
disagreement score~\citep{tarvainen2017mean}, clustering over self-supervised
features~\citep{oquab2024dinov2} so the budget spreads rather than piling onto near-duplicates. The
composition is ours, and we could not make it work.

\textbf{The reason turned out to be more interesting than the failure.} A targeted training set
really is measurably different from a randomly chosen one of the same size --- train a probe to tell
the two apart and it succeeds easily, on images it has never seen. But that difference does not come
from the aiming. It comes from the fact that aiming \emph{concentrates} the budget on a few
clusters, and any rule that concentrates it the same way reproduces the effect: pointing the budget
at the wrong clusters reproduces it, and so does reversing the uncertainty signal outright. What
looked like the effect of aiming was the effect of concentrating, which means the control that
settles the question is not a random baseline but a same-shape allocation with the signal destroyed
inside it.

On trained accuracy, the direction of the signal moves nothing this design can resolve. That null is
bounded rather than absolute --- an interval on the two conditions actually compared excludes the
improvement this approach reports, while admitting effects a few times smaller --- and it is
defended rather than asserted. A positive control confirms the measurement detects a real effect.
Substituting a perfect score for ours changes nothing. Removing the scheduling artifact that made
added images displace existing ones changes nothing. And re-running the decisive comparison at eight
seeds per condition leaves the gap \emph{smaller} than it was at three.

Separately, and independently of whether our method worked, we found that the benchmark such claims
are routinely checked against is mostly training data, and that the standard check for this reports
it clean.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The first two concern the method we built; the last two travel beyond it.

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0.04em
\item \textbf{Concentration, not the uncertainty direction}, is what makes a targeted synthetic
training set measurably different from a random one --- demonstrated in feature geometry, in trained
boundary accuracy, and across eight seeds of trained accuracy.
\item \textbf{A bounded null on the direction}, defended by five independent controls --- a positive
control, a perfect-score oracle, an unpinned optimisation schedule, a same-shape shuffle and a seed
expansion --- rather than asserted once.
\item \textbf{Three checks that cost no training} and should precede any cluster-wise acquisition
policy: whether the target data clusters at all, what the funded clusters are made of, and how much
of the endpoint's error sits between clusters rather than within them.
\item \textbf{CHAMELEON is $50$ of $76$ images ($65.8\%$) training data} for models trained on
COD10K, while exact hashing reports $0$ overlap; we release a re-encode-tolerant detector, the
confirmed pair list and a clean evaluation protocol.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
This paper does not propose a detector. It asks whether an \emph{acquisition policy} --- a rule for
deciding which synthetic images to generate under a fixed budget --- can improve a pipeline that is
otherwise held fixed. The task it does that on is camouflaged object detection, which asks for a
pixel-accurate outline of an object whose colour and texture are built to defeat exactly that;
\citet{fan2023advances} survey its architectures, datasets and evaluation conventions.

\paragraph{What we measure, and what decides.} A prediction is scored by how well its mask matches
the true one. Our primary measure is the \emph{S-measure} $S_\alpha$~\citep{fan2017structure}, which
rewards getting an object's \emph{shape} right rather than counting correct pixels, and which runs
from $0$ to $1$ with higher better. The scale matters for reading everything that follows: every
detector in this paper scores near $0.70$, so the differences the paper turns on are small fractions
of the range these models actually occupy. Three words then recur. An \textbf{arm} is one training
condition; all arms share a base pool of labelled images and differ only in which images are
appended to it. A \textbf{seed} is one training run of one arm --- repeat runs of the same arm do
not return the same score, and the size of that seed-to-seed spread is what any real difference has
to beat. An \textbf{endpoint} is a held-out test set a verdict is read from. Finally, the
\textbf{reference effect} is the improvement this repository reproduced for the published method it
builds on, $0.7030 \rightarrow 0.7172$, or $+0.0142$. That is the size of effect this design had to
be able to resolve, and the yardstick every sensitivity claim in this paper is measured against.

\paragraph{The systems this one sits on.} The closest are
LAKE-RED~\citep{zhao2024lakered}, whose background generation and compositing we use unchanged, and
S2R-COD~\citep{luo2025s2rcod}, whose synthetic-to-real self-training protocol we follow. Relative to
both, the only thing we add is an allocation layer that chooses \emph{which} generated images to
append; the generator, the budget and the training procedure are left alone. Synthetic-augmentation
methods such as DatasetDM~\citep{wu2023datasetdm} address how to construct labelled data at all,
which is a different question from how to spend a fixed amount of it.

\paragraph{Where the allocation rule comes from.} Every part of it is standard. Uncertainty sampling
comes from active learning~\citep{settles2009active}, except that our query is answered by a
generator rather than by a human annotator; the uncertainty is student--teacher disagreement of the
kind mean-teacher training already minimises~\citep{tarvainen2017mean}; and $k$-means over frozen
self-supervised features~\citep{oquab2024dinov2,radford2021learning} keeps the budget from piling
onto near-duplicates, in the spirit of core-set selection~\citep{sener2018active}. Other
constructions exist --- deep ensembles~\citep{lakshminarayanan2017simple},
MC-dropout~\citep{gal2016dropout}, BADGE~\citep{ash2020deep} --- and Section~\ref{sec:why} measures
two of them alongside ours, but we propose none. What is contributed here is a controlled test of
the composition, not a new estimator.

\paragraph{The control that is missing from this literature.} Three recent systems share the premise
that a generation budget should be steered rather than spent uniformly.
GAUDA~\citep{frisch2025gauda} conditions a latent diffusion model on the classes a Bayesian model is
least certain about; DisCL~\citep{liang2025discl} steers image-guidance strength along a curriculum;
SynQuE~\citep{chen2025synque} ranks whole synthetic datasets using only unannotated real data.
\textbf{None of the three runs a control that holds the allocation's shape fixed while destroying
the signal inside it}, and that turns out to be the control that decides the question, because a
steered selection differs from a random one in two ways at once --- where the budget points, and how
unevenly it is spread. Appendix~\ref{app:related} places these systems side by side and covers
benchmark definitions and generative alternatives. Section~\ref{sec:method} specifies the loop we
built and the arms that separate those two things.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:method}
The idea is easy to picture. Train a model on the labelled images you have; look at the unlabelled
real images it will be judged on and find the ones it handles least confidently; ask a generator for
new images resembling those, add them to the training set, and train again. What follows is our
instantiation of that idea, specified exactly, because a null result is only as informative as the
thing it tested.

\paragraph{The setting.} We train on a \emph{source pool} of $4447$ labelled image and mask pairs
and want the model to work on a \emph{target pool} of $4040$ unlabelled real images, with verdicts
read from two held-out endpoints: COD10K-test decides and NC4K is reported. The target pool is not
one distribution but a mixture of $3040$ COD10K images and $1000$ from CAMO, which matters later
(Section~\ref{sec:why}). Training runs in two rounds: a student trains alongside a teacher that is a slowly updated running
average of its own weights, target images the two already agree on are appended between rounds with
the teacher's prediction as their label, and round two retrains from scratch on the enlarged pool.
Two properties the phrase ``closed loop'' hides both bound what follows --- the round count is a
fixed constant, not a convergence criterion, and the generator renders its pool once, offline, and
is never invoked during training.

\paragraph{The allocation, in four stages.} A fixed generation budget has to be spent somewhere, and
our rule spends it where the model is weakest. \textbf{(i) Group the target images}, so that
``weak'' can name a region of the data rather than a single image: $k$-means into $k = 75$ clusters
over L2-normalised self-supervised embeddings, fitted once and never refitted. \textbf{(ii) Score
each image for uncertainty.} We need a number, computable without labels, that says how unsure the
model is; the natural candidate is how far the student and its own running average disagree, which
is available precisely because the consistency loss is already trying to reduce it:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ES}(x) \;=\; a \,\big\| \nabla \sigma(s(x)) - \nabla \sigma(t(x)) \big\|_{1}
\;+\; b \, \mathrm{BCE}\big( \sigma(s(x)),\, \sigma(t(x)) \big),
\label{eq:es}
\end{equation}
with student and teacher logits $s,t$, sigmoid $\sigma$, Sobel gradient magnitude $\nabla$, and
configured weights $a, b$ in Appendix~\ref{app:method}. What this buys is a score needing no ground
truth, computable on exactly the images the model will be judged on; a cluster's score is the mean
over its members. Its dominant term is a distance between \emph{gradient} magnitudes, which makes it
a boundary-focused signal --- a fact Section~\ref{sec:concentration} returns to. \textbf{(iii) Turn scores into a budget split.} The
budget is spread by a temperature softmax over the cluster scores, at temperature
$\atemp = 1.0$; the temperature sets how sharply the budget piles onto the worst clusters.
\textbf{(iv) Fill each quota} by drawing the foregrounds whose masked cutouts sit closest to the
funded cluster's centroid.

\paragraph{The arms, and what each one holds fixed.} All arms share the same base pool and differ
only in which $1000$ images are appended to it, so any difference between two of them is
attributable to that choice and nothing else. \textbf{A0} appends
nothing. \textbf{A2} appends $1000$ real photographs, which separates the effect of a larger pool
from the effect of generated content. \textbf{B} appends $1000$ renders drawn uniformly at random,
redrawn per seed. \textbf{C10} appends the $1000$ that the allocation above selects. A2, B and C10
all hold $5447$ pairs, and the comparison this paper turns on is C10 against B.

That comparison cannot settle the question on its own, and Figure~\ref{fig:arms} is why: a targeted
allocation differs from a random one along \emph{two} axes at once --- it is more concentrated, and
it points somewhere --- so a difference between C10 and B is attributable to neither. Three further
arms hold concentration exactly fixed and vary only where the budget points. \textbf{CSHUF} permutes
the cluster scores, destroying the signal while preserving the allocation's shape; \textbf{CINV}
reverses their rank; \textbf{CORACLE} replaces the score with each cluster's true endpoint error.

\paragraph{What decides.} Write $\hat{\sigma}$ for the seed-to-seed wobble --- the pooled within-arm
standard deviation of $S_\alpha$, taken at the conventional $\alpha = 0.5$. A gap is a
\textsc{real effect} only if it exceeds twice that wobble \emph{and} every seed agrees on
its sign; a smaller gap is \textsc{within noise}; one that clears the bar without agreeing in sign is
\textsc{inconclusive}, and is reported as such rather than resolved by adding seeds. The rule applies
per architecture, and a disagreement between them is reported as a disagreement. We report no
$p$-values at three seeds. Appendix~\ref{app:method} records the rule's provenance, the seeds, and
the gates each campaign passed before launching.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
Four analyses follow, each measured independently of the others: whether the targeted allocation
beats a random one on trained accuracy, what makes a targeted set measurably different regardless of
that null, the mechanism behind the null, and a separate finding about the benchmark used to read all
of the above.

\subsection{Does Targeting Beat Random?}
\label{sec:null}
The loop is specified, so the first question is the one it was built to answer. \textbf{Aiming the
budget by uncertainty did not beat spending it at random.} On the primary endpoint at eight seeds
per arm, the gap between the targeted allocation and a random one is $+0.0024$ --- about a third of
the spread it would have had to clear --- and every one of the sixteen gaps that campaign decides
returns the same verdict.

A null is only as good as the objections it survives, so the rest of this section is those
objections and what each one was answered with. Table~\ref{tab:trained} is the whole trained record:
four campaigns that return nothing, and one that returns something, which is how we know the fourth
column means anything at all.

\paragraph{More seeds made the effect smaller, not the measurement finer.} That is the plain form of
what the seed expansion returned, and it is worth stating before the arithmetic, because the
arithmetic looks backwards. Eight seeds per arm give a truer estimate of the seed-to-seed spread
than three did: the real spread is \emph{larger} than the optimistic three-seed guess, while the gap
itself halved, from $+0.0050$ to $+0.0024$. So the bar sits at $0.0069$ on $28$ degrees of freedom
rather than the $0.0034$ the pre-registration projected --- wider, on three and a half times the
evidence about how noisy this training actually is --- and the gap now sits inside even the
tightest bar this project has measured, $0.0055$, which at three seeds it did not.
Appendix~\ref{app:diag} gives the per-arm spreads and the projection this replaces.

\paragraph{Three objections, three measurements.} Each of the following is a reason the null might
be an artifact rather than a result, and each has a campaign attached rather than a concession.

\emph{Perhaps we simply chose a bad uncertainty estimator.} The oracle campaign replaces the score
with each cluster's \emph{true} error on the endpoint --- information no real method could have ---
and nothing moves: all eight of its gaps are within noise and not one is even directionally stable.
The substitution was not a small one, either. Our score and the truth it stands in for agree at a
rank correlation of $+0.2372$, so we replaced the signal with something several times better aimed
and the trained result did not notice. This converts \emph{our score did not help} into the stronger
\emph{no score of any quality helped here}.

\emph{Perhaps the added images were never really trained on.} They were not, under the committed
schedule: steps per epoch are set by the shorter of two data loaders, so appended images displace
base-pool exposure instead of adding to it. The FX campaign removes that pin, which raises steps per
epoch from $253$ to $341$ on one architecture and $127$ to $171$ on the other. The decisive gap
stays within noise in all four cells and \emph{shrinks}, to $+0.0014$. Growing the budget did not
reveal an effect the pin was hiding.

\emph{Perhaps three seeds is too few.} It was, and that is what the seed expansion above measures.
It is a separate campaign with its own arms, noise estimate and verdicts rather than extra seeds
bolted onto a finished one, because adding seeds after seeing a result is how a null gets talked
into existence.

\paragraph{The instrument is not blind.} A null is only worth reading if the same measurement can
detect something real, so we trained an arm we expected to be clearly worse --- a mean-teacher
baseline, same architecture, same seeds, same scorer, same rule --- and asked whether this design
would see the difference. It did, comfortably and with every seed agreeing.
\textbf{And that control does not close the gap that matters most.} It shows an effect of roughly
the reference magnitude is visible; it does not show that an effect the size of our nulls would be.
The control's own bar, $0.0060$, is itself larger than the decisive gap it is meant to reassure us
about, so a positive control at $0.02$ licenses \emph{the instrument works}, not \emph{it would have
seen the effect we are nulling}.

\paragraph{What the measurement can and cannot rule out.} The decisive statement is the
pre-registered one above: no effect on the primary cell larger than the bar the seed expansion
measured. A post-hoc interval on the two arms actually compared is tighter still,
placing the gap in $[-0.0020, +0.0067]$, which \textbf{excludes the reference effect by more than a
factor of two} while admitting effects a few times smaller. That interval decides nothing --- the
frozen rule admits no interval --- but it is a stronger honest statement than ``we could not have
seen it''. At eight seeds a formal equivalence test also becomes informative for the first time, and
Section~\ref{sec:concentration} reports it where the asymmetry it reveals belongs.

\subsection{Concentration, Not Targeting}
\label{sec:concentration}
The null says the direction of the signal did not move the endpoint. It does not say that targeting
changes nothing --- and it plainly does change something, because a targeted training set is easy to
tell apart from a random one. \textbf{What makes it different is that the budget is concentrated on
a few clusters, not that it is aimed at the right ones.} Three independent measurements say so: the
geometry of the selected set, trained accuracy at eight seeds, and the one family of metrics on
which the effect of concentration is large enough to resolve.

\paragraph{The geometry: every rule that concentrates reproduces the separation.} Aim a generation
budget at the clusters where the model is least confident and the training set you get really does
look different from a randomly chosen one of the same size --- train a probe to tell the two apart
and it succeeds easily, on images it has never seen. But re-run the selection with the uncertainty
scores shuffled between clusters, so the budget is spread exactly as unevenly while pointing at the
wrong places, and the separation survives. Aim at an arbitrary cluster instead: it survives again.
Only removing the unevenness itself, by drawing two random sets of equal size, collapses it
(Figure~\ref{fig:thesis}a). The probe was detecting concentration all along.

Paired across twenty cells of budget and configuration, the real signal contributes $+0.0073$ of a
Cohen's $d$ over its own shuffle --- and against an arbitrary cluster it is \emph{negative}, at
$-0.0649$. On the strongest directional evidence we have, aiming does slightly worse than not
aiming. What targeting buys is narrowness rather than reach: an effective-rank ratio of $0.634$ in
every cell tested, covering no more of the target manifold than a random draw.

\paragraph{Trained accuracy: reversing the signal does not hurt.} Geometry is not performance, so we
trained it. The falsification arms hold the allocation's shape fixed to five decimal places and vary
only where the budget points, and at eight seeds per arm the arm that \emph{reverses} the
uncertainty signal --- aiming the budget at the clusters the score calls easiest --- has the highest
mean score in \textbf{four of the four} architecture--endpoint cells (Figure~\ref{fig:thesis}b).
This is not a claim that reversal helps --- every one of those differences is within noise. It is
the absence of an ordering: if direction were what mattered, reversing it would cost something, and
nothing is paid. Even the destroyed-against-reversed contrast, which carries the strongest sign
agreement in the campaign at $8/8$ on one cell, does not reach two-thirds of its bar.

At eight seeds a formal equivalence test becomes informative for the first time, and which gaps it
resolves is telling. Five of sixteen are equivalent within $\pm 0.005$, and they are the
\emph{direction} contrasts: targeted against its own shuffle rejects at $p = 0.019$, while the
targeting-versus-random gap does not reject at all ($p = 0.101$). Direction is equivalent;
concentration is not --- the same asymmetry, in statistical form. These tests are post-hoc and
decide nothing.

\paragraph{Boundary metrics: where concentration is resolvable, reversal captures all of it.} The
acquisition signal of Equation~\ref{eq:es} is boundary-focused, but $S_\alpha$ is structure-aware
rather than boundary-specific, so the campaign never measured the quantity the signal was built to
move. Re-scoring every run's committed predictions with two boundary metrics closes that gap
(Table~\ref{tab:boundary}), and on SINet the concentration effect finally clears its bar. So does
the anti-targeted arm, at $1.34\times$ against targeted selection's $1.37\times$ --- the same effect
to within a tenth of a bar --- while the two direction contrasts stay far inside it.
\textbf{This is the strongest form of the result we have}, because it is the one place where the
same instrument resolves concentration and fails to resolve direction \emph{in the same cells}: the
null on direction is not simply an absence of sensitivity.

\subsection{Why It Fails}
\label{sec:why}
Concentration explains why targeting \emph{looks} like it works. It does not explain why this loop
could never have delivered the thing it promised, and that has a mechanism --- one chain, measured
end to end before any of the training runs above were scored.

\textbf{The target barely clusters.} Under every embedding we tried, $k$-means separation peaks at a
silhouette of $0.1600$ and is lower in the other two spaces --- far below any conventional threshold
for calling a partition real. So a cluster-wise policy is drawing lines the data does not
support; \textbf{and even where those lines are real, the error does not sit between them.} Only
about a fifth of the endpoint's structural-error variance is between-cluster at all, so four fifths
of what a targeted policy would have to fix is invisible to \emph{any} cluster-level allocation,
however well aimed.

\textbf{What the budget does reach is not the endpoint but one half of a mixture.} CAMO images are
$24.8\%$ of the target pool, and the allocation is not neutral between the two components: $51.5\%$
of the budget lands on them, while the two largest quotas together hold $7$ of the $2026$ images the
paper is scored on. ``Allocate by uncertainty'' turns out to be, in substantial part, ``allocate
to CAMO'' --- \textbf{and the quantity doing the allocating is itself the wrong one.} The
uncertainty score tracks per-pixel error about twice as strongly as it tracks the structural error
the endpoint measures ($+0.8553$ against $+0.4276$), and that ordering holds for three independent
uncertainty estimators on both architectures, so it is a property of uncertainty-guided allocation
here rather than a quirk of our particular score. Partialling out object area, the most plausible
mechanical explanation, \emph{raises} the structural correlation in every row --- the opposite of
what the confound predicts.

\textbf{And beneath all of it, the generator supplies nothing new to allocate over.} It paints
backgrounds and copies objects: the render set is a bijection onto the base foregrounds and not one
of $8885$ traced objects shows any sign of having been regenerated. The distribution it produces is
narrower than its own input --- synthetic images reach $0.13$ to $0.54$ of the real target
distribution, while the generator's own source photographs, real pictures of an entirely different
genre, reach $0.71$ to $0.75$. A policy allocating over that pool is choosing among copies.

\textbf{One pattern points the other way, and we report it.} Pool \emph{size} may matter where pool
\emph{content} does not: the unpadded arm trails the padded ones consistently, reaching a
\textsc{real effect} on the secondary endpoint and two \textsc{inconclusive} verdicts once the
schedule is unpinned. No single step clears its bar and we claim nothing from it, but it points away
from our conclusion, which is why it is here. Separately, we withdrew one of our own candidate
causes: we suspected the generator's conditioning channel was too narrow to act on a detailed
instruction, read the released source, and found four routes carrying foreground information with
the dominant one bypassing the bottleneck we had blamed --- so the explanation was wrong and is
retired rather than left standing. Appendix~\ref{app:tables} gives the full clustering sweep, the
signal-generality table and the area control.

\subsection{CHAMELEON Is Mostly Training Data}
\label{sec:contamination}
The causes above are about our loop. This one is about the benchmark everyone uses, and it holds
whether or not our loop worked.

\textbf{Fifty of CHAMELEON's $76$ images --- $65.8\%$ --- are the same photographs as images in the
COD10K-train and CAMO training pools}, re-encoded, rescaled or cropped. Forty-four have their
partner in the public COD10K-train split. A model trained on that split has therefore already seen
most of CHAMELEON before being evaluated on it. The standard check does not notice: hashing the
files returns \emph{zero} overlap, and it is not wrong --- the copies were re-saved in a different
format, so not one byte matches while every pixel does. Sixteen images resolve neither way and are
reported \textsc{unchecked} rather than clean, so $50$ is a floor rather than an estimate.

\textbf{The count is measured in two tiers} (Figure~\ref{fig:cham}). The floor is what a released
same-dimension detector reproduces on any machine: $41$ images whose partner shares their exact
pixel dimensions and differs only by re-encoding. A rescaled or cropped copy leaves its dimension
group and is invisible to that detector, so we recover those by matching keypoints under a geometric
model and then --- because agreeing on a homography is not the same as being one photograph ---
warping each candidate into the CHAMELEON frame and measuring the residual. \textbf{Nine survive
that test}; one cleared the keypoint threshold, failed the residual, and is excluded. Thresholds are
calibrated on pairs whose answer is already committed, the finding is verified against a
byte-identical author-sourced release, and the same detector pointed at NC4K flags $0$ of $120$
(Appendix~\ref{app:chamext}).

\textbf{We claim non-independence, not inflation.} We expected the contamination to flatter
CHAMELEON scores, measured it, and did not find it: all eight difficulty percentiles for the leaked
subset fall between $0.448$ and $0.559$. A protocol violation does not need a score advantage to
disqualify a benchmark. We also withdraw our own earlier recommendation to report CHAMELEON on its
uncontaminated subset, because that subset is now $10$ images, one of which is byte-identical to an
image in the split we select checkpoints on. \textbf{A ten-image endpoint is not an endpoint:
report COD10K-test and NC4K instead, and disclose the unchecked share.}\footnote{This is not
hypothetical for the protocol we build on, which designates the real unlabelled COD10K training set
as its target domain. Testing on CHAMELEON, it removes those images from the \emph{source} domain
--- closing that path but not the target pool, which holds the same $44$. We attribute no intent:
the source-side removal shows the authors were alert to leakage, and the target-side path is
invisible without a re-encode-tolerant check.} The detector, the pair list and the clean protocol
are released and run without any of our data.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Two kinds of evidence sit underneath this paper and should not be run together. The
\emph{geometric} result is positive, and we state it without hedging: concentration, not the
direction of the uncertainty signal, produces the separation that makes targeted selection look like
it works. The \emph{downstream} result is a null under stated sensitivity --- it does not show that
uncertainty guidance cannot work, and we have named the size at which it would have.

Four things transfer beyond this setup. \textbf{Three checks cost no training and should precede any
cluster-wise allocation:} whether the data separates into clusters at all, what the funded clusters
are made of relative to the pool they are drawn from, and how much of the endpoint's error sits
between clusters rather than within them. \textbf{The discriminating control is not a random
baseline} but a same-shape allocation with the signal destroyed inside it, because random selection
differs from targeting in two ways at once. \textbf{A null on the endpoint metric does not license a
null on the quantity the acquisition signal was built to move.} And \textbf{a real-versus-synthetic
AUC near $1.0$ is near-vacuous evidence} --- a lossy re-save of the identical images separates at
$0.9928$.

The idea still seems reasonable. For it to have a chance you would need an optimisation budget that
grows with the pool rather than one under which added images displace real ones, a foreground supply
not already exhausted, a generator wider than its own input, and a target pool that is not a mixture
whose components the signal can tell apart. Nothing here says closed-loop generation cannot work,
only that this build could not have shown that it does. One pattern runs through the method and the
benchmark alike: \textbf{a control that returns zero is informative only if it could have returned
something else.}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
The question this paper set out to answer was narrow: under a fixed synthetic-data budget, does
aiming generation at what the model finds hardest beat spending the same budget at random? Across
$103$ trained runs and five controls that each close a different way the null could be an artifact,
it does not, at the tightest bar this project could measure. The reason is worth carrying forward on
its own: a targeted allocation \emph{is} measurably different from a random one, but only because it
concentrates the budget, not because it aims it, and that distinction is invisible to any comparison
that omits a same-shape control. A second and independently motivated audit found that the field's
oldest camouflage benchmark is two-thirds training data under an exact-byte check that a routine
re-encoding defeats. Together the two results argue for the same practice: before crediting an
acquisition rule or trusting a duplicate check, build the version of the test that could have
failed, and see whether it does.

\section{Additional Related Work}
\label{app:related}

\paragraph{COD benchmarks and detectors.}
The standard COD evaluation suite comprises COD10K~\cite{fan2022concealed}, a large benchmark
with ten camouflage categories and pixel-accurate masks; CAMO~\cite{le2019anabranch}, which focuses
on man-made camouflage and occlusion; NC4K~\cite{lv2021simultaneously}, assembled from Flickr; and
CHAMELEON~\cite{skurowski2018animal}, an earlier unpublished collection that has become a de~facto
standard. The continued use of all four matters for Section~\ref{sec:contamination}: an image shared
by a training set and any reported test set contaminates evaluation for methods using the full suite.
SINet~\cite{fan2020sinet} decomposes COD into search and identification branches, while SINet-v2
\cite{fan2022concealed} adds a neighbour connection decoder for boundary refinement; we use both
off-the-shelf. SegMaR~\cite{jia2022segment} iteratively magnifies and re-processes ambiguous
regions, representing architectural progress outside our allocation experiment. We report the
structure-aware S-measure~\cite{fan2017structure}, enhanced-alignment E-measure
\cite{fan2018enhanced}, and weighted $F_\beta^w$~\cite{margolin2014evaluate} for comparability
with S2R-COD.

\paragraph{Synthetic generation and domain adaptation.}
Latent diffusion~\cite{rombach2022high} and DDIM~\cite{song2021denoising} provide the high-resolution
generator and deterministic sampling schedule inherited by our pipeline. Copy-paste augmentation
\cite{ghiasi2021simple} similarly composes object cut-outs with varied backgrounds; LAKE-RED applies
this compositional idea while retrieving background patches. CyCADA~\cite{hoffman2018cycada}, in
contrast, aligns source and target feature distributions with cycle-consistent adversarial training;
our loop does not operate at the feature level and instead feeds generator output into a
self-training objective. Other camouflage-image generators exist~\cite{chen2025realcamo}, but they
are outside the experiment, so the reported null is bounded to LAKE-RED.

\paragraph{Uncertainty-guided acquisition and self-training.}
FixMatch~\cite{sohn2020fixmatch} couples confidence thresholding with consistency regularisation;
our pseudo-labels likewise arise in an inter-round consistency step, but drive a second training
round rather than an online update. Pseudo-labelling~\cite{lee2013pseudo} supplies the hard-target
view of this step. DINOv2~\cite{oquab2024dinov2} and CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning} provide the
frozen representations used for clustering. These choices complement core-set selection
\cite{sener2018active}: the clusters spread the budget, while the disagreement scores determine
which clusters receive more of it. BADGE~\cite{ash2020deep} jointly uses uncertainty and diversity
in gradient-embedding space; our same-shape shuffle in Section~\ref{sec:concentration} is a direct
control for the corresponding entanglement in this allocation.

\paragraph{Benchmark integrity and negative results.}
Dataset-collection procedures can induce shared statistical bias~\cite{torralba2011unbiased}, and
near-duplicate analyses of CIFAR and ImageNet show that visually identical images can cross split
boundaries~\cite{barz2020train}. Related contamination concerns appear in language-model evaluation
\cite{wu2024antileak}; the mechanism differs here, but the need for explicit auditing is shared.
Our pre-registration follows reproducibility guidance~\cite{pineau2021improving} and the case for
publishing disconfirming results~\cite{karl2024position}. Finally, criticisms of learned comparison
measures under resizing and representation choices~\cite{parmar2022aliased,jayasumana2024rethinking}
motivate treating encoding-sensitive probes as diagnostics rather than as clean perceptual evidence.

three systems discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related} beside this one. The last column is the point:
it records whether a signal-destroying or same-shape control is run, which is what separates an
effect of \emph{aiming} a budget from an effect of \emph{concentrating} it. It is not a claim of
superiority --- our own entry records a null.

\section{Detailed Method Specification}
\label{app:method}

\paragraph{Training.} SINet~\citep{fan2020sinet} at batch $16$ for $40$ epochs and
SINet-v2~\citep{fan2022concealed} at batch $32$ for $100$ epochs, Adam at $10^{-4}$, images at
$352 \times 352$ with ImageNet normalisation. The EMA teacher uses momentum $0.996$ under this task
setting. The consistency weights in Equation~\ref{eq:es} are the configured $a = 0.9$, $b = 0.3$
with the unweighted cross-entropy variant --- these are the values the trainer sets for this task,
not the loss class's own defaults. Strong views add random autocontrast and Gaussian blur to the
weak view. \texttt{cudnn.deterministic} is enabled and \texttt{cudnn.benchmark} disabled, which
reduces, and is not claimed to eliminate, kernel nondeterminism.

\paragraph{Seeds and what was held constant.} The committed seeds are $\{42, 43, 45\}$; the seed
expansion of Section~\ref{sec:null} adds $\{46, 47, 48, 49, 50\}$, fixed in writing before its first
run. Across the four-arm campaign's $24$ runs the base pool is asserted byte-identical against a
hash manifest with $0$ mismatches, both rounds read the unfiltered $4040$-image target set, and
every run shares one trainer invocation, checkpoint rule, endpoint set and deterministic-kernel
setting.

\paragraph{Optimisation budget.} Steps per epoch are set by the shorter of the source and target
loaders, which pins them at $253$ (SINet) and $127$ (SINet-v2) in both rounds of every committed run
regardless of pool size, so appended images displace base-pool exposure rather than adding to it.
This is measured per run and asserted rather than assumed. It is not an asymmetry between the arms
that matter --- B and C10 hold identical pool sizes and therefore receive identical step counts ---
and Section~\ref{sec:null} reports the campaign that removes the pin and re-runs the comparison
without it.

\paragraph{Constructing the falsification arms.} CSHUF's permutation is the \textbf{first} of $64$
pre-declared draws satisfying zero fixed points and $|\rho| \leq 0.10$, chosen by a rule fixed
before the draw and never by inspecting its consequences; its realised rank correlation with the
true score vector is $-0.03351$, against CINV's exact $-1.0$. CINV's single fixed point is
mathematically necessary: at odd $k$ the median-ranked cluster is its own mirror. Because equality
is the \emph{supporting} outcome for these arms, two near-identical arms would manufacture it, so
set distinctness was gated before any GPU time at a Jaccard bound of $0.50$: between $452$ and $511$
of each pair's $1000$ injected images differ, at $2.2$--$2.4\times$ chance overlap.

\paragraph{Allocation sweeps.} Stage (iv) matches against a grey-masked cutout rather than a render,
because at selection time the foreground exists and the render does not. The budget grid is
$B \in \{250, 500, 1000, 2000, 3000, 4447\}$ and the temperature grid over $\atemp$ is extended
until both degenerate ends are reached --- concentrated when $\max_j p_j \geq 0.95$, uniform when
total variation from uniform falls below $0.01$ --- with caps at $\atemp \in [10^{-4}, 10^{3}]$. The
campaign uses $\atemp = 1.0$, $B = 1000$.

\paragraph{Metrics.} $S_\alpha$ at $\alpha = 0.5$ is the primary endpoint metric; MAE is the
checkpoint-selection metric; $F_\beta^w$ and $E_\phi$ are computed and reported but decide nothing.
The $F$-measures apply $\beta$ in the non-squared form, so the metric name rather than a formula is
what identifies them.

\paragraph{Degrees of freedom.} Each campaign's $\hat{\sigma}$ is pooled within-arm across that
campaign's own arms, at $\mathrm{df} = (\text{arms}) \times (\text{seeds} - 1)$: $8$ for the
three-seed campaigns over four arms, $6$ for the three-arm controls, and $28$ for the eight-seed
expansion. A campaign's bar is never swapped for another campaign's in either direction.

\paragraph{The A2 control's one confound.} A2 pairs the authors' original photographs with the
authors' masks, whose mean foreground fraction differs from the raw masks by $0.0058$ on $1000$ of
$5447$ images. This affects $\Delta(\mathrm{B}-\mathrm{A2})$ and is disclosed; it does not touch
$\Delta(\mathrm{C10}-\mathrm{B})$, the decisive gap, since B and C10 share mask provenance, pool
size and render pool.

\paragraph{Pre-registration commits and amendments.} Every decision rule was committed before the
runs it governs. The four-arm rule is recorded at \texttt{065dac6}, the commit carrying that
campaign's first log block; the falsification campaign's at \texttt{617a2e1}; the signal-generality
check's at \texttt{6efde9f}; the area control's and the positive control's at \texttt{c2114af}; the
seed expansion's at \texttt{b755aa9}, dated 2026-09-18. The oracle and optimisation-schedule
campaigns carry their own pre-registrations, each committed before that campaign's first training
run. Three amendments exist, all append-only and dated. Two are reference-tolerance defects of the
same kind: a frozen clause requiring reproduction to better than $10^{-9}$ against a file recording
six decimal places, and --- in the area control, written by the same author who had just documented
the first --- the identical mistake against a file recording four. Both were replaced by exact
agreement at the recorded precision, which is the strictest satisfiable form, and neither altered a
decision threshold. The third marks one verification gate not applicable in scope after it was found
to compare two architectures' signals.

\paragraph{Why the committed bar is so wide, and why we did not narrow it.} The four-arm campaign
pools its within-arm spread across all four of its arms, and one of them dominates. On the primary
cell the per-arm standard deviations are A0 $0.01727$, A2 $0.00193$, B $0.00181$ and C10 $0.00406$:
\textbf{A0 alone contributes $92.7\%$ of the pooled variance}, and A0 appears in the decisive
comparison not at all. Its instability is unexplained and is reported rather than excluded, because
the bar was pre-registered as a pooled quantity and re-deriving it after seeing the result is
precisely the move the pre-registration exists to prevent. What we do instead is report the tighter
bars that later campaigns measured on their own arms, and let the reader see all of them.

\paragraph{The three bars, and what each is good for.} Three estimates of the same wobble exist,
and they are not interchangeable. The committed four-arm bar is $0.0179$ and is inflated by A0 as
above. The falsification campaign, pooling over B, C10, CSHUF and CINV --- the arms that actually
appear in a decisive gap --- measures $0.0055$ at the same degrees of freedom. The seed expansion
measures $0.0069$ over the same four arms at $28$ degrees of freedom instead of $8$. The asymmetry
that matters is this: where equality is the \emph{supporting} outcome, a tighter bar is
conservative, but here the claim and the null coincide, so a looser bar flatters us. That is why the
paper reads the gap against the tightest defensible bar rather than the widest, and why the seed
expansion's bar --- not the committed one --- is the sensitivity statement in Section~\ref{sec:null}.

\paragraph{The bar widened when the seeds grew, and that is the expected behaviour.} The seed
expansion's pre-registration projected that eight seeds would tighten the bar by
$\sqrt{8/3} \approx 1.63\times$, \emph{if the per-arm spreads stayed as measured}. They did not.
Every one grew: on the primary cell B went $0.00181 \rightarrow 0.00251$, C10
$0.00406 \rightarrow 0.00489$, CSHUF $0.00166 \rightarrow 0.00278$ and CINV
$0.00285 \rightarrow 0.00302$. A pooled standard deviation on eight degrees of freedom is a noisy
estimate that lands low as often as high; on twenty-eight it does not. So the expansion did not buy
a smaller bar, it bought a trustworthy one --- and in doing so showed the three-seed bars to have
been optimistic rather than conservative.

\paragraph{The interval at three seeds, and why it left the main text.} A Welch interval on the two
arms actually compared, at the committed three seeds, places the decisive gap in
$[-0.0035, +0.0136]$ and excludes the reference effect. It is superseded by the eight-seed interval
reported in Section~\ref{sec:null} and is recorded here only for continuity with the committed
account: at roughly $2.8$ degrees of freedom its exclusion held by a margin too thin to lean on,
which is exactly the weakness the seed expansion removes.

\paragraph{Boundary metrics: the binarisation sweep in full.} Boundary metrics binarise a
prediction where $S_\alpha$ does not, so the result in Section~\ref{sec:concentration} could in
principle be an artifact of where the threshold sits. It is not. Swept over thresholds
$0.4 / 0.5 / 0.6$ on SINet Boundary-IoU, $\Delta(\mathrm{C10}-\mathrm{B})$ holds at
$1.39 / 1.37 / 1.23 \times$ its bar and $\Delta(\mathrm{CINV}-\mathrm{B})$ at
$1.25 / 1.34 / 1.26 \times$, both $3/3$ sign-consistent at every threshold, while the two direction
contrasts never exceed $0.52\times$ and $0.14\times$ anywhere in the sweep. Concentration resolves
at every threshold; direction resolves at none.

\paragraph{How much the trained comparison is attenuated.} The trained half of the concentration
result is diluted three times over, and we disclose it because the attenuation biases toward the
null we report. Injected images are $1000$ of $5447$ pairs, or $18.4\%$ of the pool; under the
committed schedule the pinned step count dilutes their exposure further; and only about $48\%$ of
injected images differ between any two arms, giving an effective contrast near $8.8\%$. Two of the
three are now measured rather than argued: the optimisation-schedule campaign removes the pin and
the decisive gap \emph{shrinks}, and the seed expansion triples the degrees of freedom behind the
bar. What remains is the third, which is a property of comparing two arms that share $89\%$ of
their training data by construction.

\section{CHAMELEON Contamination: Calibration, Controls and Adjudication}
\label{app:chamext}

\paragraph{Tier A --- the released same-dimension detector.} Images are grouped by exact pixel
dimensions, since a re-encode preserves them. Within a group each image is reduced to a
$32\times32$ greyscale descriptor, pairs are shortlisted by descriptor RMS distance at $14.0$, and
survivors are decoded at full resolution and confirmed when the mean absolute difference over RGB
falls below $6/255$. We deliberately do \emph{not} contrast-normalise the descriptor: normalising
destroys the scale that separates a re-encode from a merely similar photograph, and it is what made
our own first pass report $10$ of $76$ rather than $41$. Sweeping the tolerance over
$1, 2, 3, 5, 6$ gives $11$, $26$, $37$, $40$ and $41$ --- the count saturates. The detector passes an
eight-assertion synthetic known-answer test that uses no repository data.

\paragraph{Tier B --- calibration of the geometric extension.} SIFT correspondences, Lowe ratio
$0.75$, filtered by a RANSAC homography at $4$ px. Thresholds are fixed on data whose answer is
already committed, before the unchecked images are scored: positives are the $41$ Tier-A pairs,
negatives $4000$ random CHAMELEON$\times$training pairs.

\section{CHAMELEON Mask Releases and Detector Validation}
\label{app:masks}

The two circulating CHAMELEON ground-truth releases are different annotations, unrelated to
contamination or to any method. They disagree in polarity, giving a mean IoU of $0.0$ as stored and
$0.6932$ after polarity alignment; only $27$ of $76$ masks are exactly identical after alignment and
$40$ of $76$ reach IoU $\geq 0.9$. On identical predictions the choice of release alone moves MAE by
$2.7\times$ ($0.2196$ against the author-sourced masks, $0.0816$ against the repackaged ground truth).
Any CHAMELEON column is therefore not comparable across papers unless the release is named.

\paragraph{Detector self-test.} The released detector passes an eight-assertion synthetic
known-answer test using no repository data, reproduces the canonical $41$ pairs and $41$ names
exactly, and surfaced one additional pair --- the second partner of the one image that has two.

\section{The Three Controls: Execution Records and Disclosures}
\label{app:controls}

Section~\ref{sec:null} closes three objections with three campaigns. Each carries a disclosure that
belongs with it.

\paragraph{The oracle campaign uses test labels, deliberately.} CORACLE takes the committed
allocation's shape and re-assigns it across clusters by the rank of each cluster's \emph{true} error
on the endpoint. That is information no real method could have, and the pre-registration says so
before the first run: it is a diagnostic upper bound and never a method, and a pre-flight gate that
would forbid this for any real arm is passed on a technicality, which we disclose rather than claim
as cleanliness. One further limitation: the oracle is derived from one architecture's errors and
applied to both. The two architectures' per-cluster errors agree at a rank correlation of $0.9211$,
so the second architecture receives a near-oracle rather than a perfect one.

\paragraph{The schedule campaign bundles four changes, not one.} Unpinning the optimisation budget
does not isolate a single variable. It raises steps per epoch from $253$ to $341$ (SINet) and $127$
to $171$ (SINet-v2); it re-uses the same $4040$ target images more often, so each is seen about
$1.35$ times per epoch instead of once; it leaves the learning-rate schedule unrescaled, because
rescaling would re-impose a fixed budget and defeat the control; and it therefore changes total
optimisation as well as its distribution. Growing the budget by roughly a third made every arm
slightly \emph{worse} --- $11$ of $12$ cross-schedule comparisons are negative --- most plausibly
because the learning rate decays per epoch and was deliberately left alone. A gate declared in
advance that any run whose step count stayed at the old pinned value was a halt rather than a
result.

\paragraph{The seed expansion is a separate campaign, and survived a power loss.} It is not the
committed campaign with more seeds: adding seeds after seeing a result is optional stopping, and it
would invalidate every verdict the original campaign reports. SE has its own four arms, its own
noise estimate, its own bar and its own verdicts, and its eight seeds were fixed in writing before
its first run. The twelve runs at the committed seeds are re-scored rather than re-trained, and must
reproduce their committed values to six decimal places or the campaign halts.

A host power loss on 2026-09-20 interrupted training. Two runs were killed part-way through their
first round; the driver's own pre-launch check discarded both and re-ran them from scratch, and they
are the only two entries in the discard record. No partially trained model enters the scoring pool,
no seed was added or dropped, and no SE number existed at the moment resumption was decided --- the
metrics file was absent, so nothing had been computed and nothing seen. The campaign cost $75.9$
GPU-hours across its $40$ trained runs.

\paragraph{What differs between arms beyond the injected images.} The pseudo-labelling step between
rounds selects target images using each arm's \emph{own} first-round model, so each arm appends a
different number of differently-labelled images. Across the seed expansion the means are $1977$ (B),
$2025$ (C10), $1990$ (CSHUF) and $1979$ (CINV), with per-run values spanning $1817$ to $2259$. This
is legitimately part of ``closed loop'' and is reported rather than absorbed: it is a second place
the arms differ beyond the injection, and it is high-variance. The step count stays pinned
regardless, which is asserted per run.
